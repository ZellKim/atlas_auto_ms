## 1. Introduction

The vermiform appendix has long been characterized as a vestigial organ since Charles Darwin's description in 1871. However, accumulating evidence from comparative anatomy, immunology, and microbiology has fundamentally challenged this view, revealing the appendix as a functionally significant component of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT).

Histologically, the appendix is densely infiltrated with lymphoid tissue analogous to Peyer's patches and the avian bursa of Fabricius. It harbors abundant B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, and natural killer cells, and serves as a major site of immunoglobulin A (IgA) induction. Notably, IgM⁺IgD⁻ B cells constitute approximately 30% of appendiceal B cells—substantially higher than the less than 5% observed in circulation—and these cells exhibit memory features with the capacity for class-switching to IgA in response to both T-dependent and T-independent stimulation. The appendix is thus positioned as a critical organ for the maturation and dissemination of B lymphocytes throughout the intestinal mucosal immune system.

Beyond its immunological role, the appendix has been proposed to function as a "safe house" for commensal gut bacteria, maintaining a biofilm reservoir of the intestinal microbiome. This biofilm inoculum facilitates the re-colonization of the proximal colon following perturbations such as antibiotic use, dietary changes, or infectious colitis. Studies have demonstrated that patients who underwent incidental or prophylactic appendectomy exhibit reduced richness and diversity of gut microbiota, supporting the hypothesis that the appendix contributes to maintaining microbial homeostasis.

Given these dual functions—immune modulation and microbial reservoir—the potential long-term consequences of appendectomy have become a subject of considerable clinical interest. Appendectomy remains one of the most commonly performed emergency surgical procedures worldwide, yet its long-term immunological and metabolic sequelae have not been fully elucidated.

### Appendectomy and Associated Diseases: Current Evidence

Several lines of epidemiological evidence have linked appendectomy to the subsequent development of various diseases, though the strength and direction of these associations remain debated.

**Inflammatory bowel disease.** The relationship between appendectomy and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has been the most extensively studied. A substantial body of evidence suggests that appendectomy is protective against ulcerative colitis (UC), particularly when performed before age 20 for acute appendicitis. The proposed mechanism involves the appendix acting as a "priming station" for T-helper responses, with its removal shifting the balance toward suppressor T-cell function. Recent experimental evidence has further clarified this relationship: appendiceal B lymphocytes have been shown to exacerbate colonic inflammation by migrating to the colon via the CCL20-CCR6 axis and facilitating Th1 and Th17 immune responses, providing a mechanistic basis for the therapeutic potential of appendectomy in UC. In contrast, appendectomy has been consistently associated with an increased risk of Crohn's disease (CD), with meta-analyses reporting a relative risk of 1.61 (95% CI 1.28–2.02), although diagnostic bias and shared genetic factors may partially account for this association.

**Clostridioides difficile infection.** The "safe house" hypothesis receives support from studies on C. difficile infection (CDI), where the absence of the appendix has been associated with increased severity, recurrence, and need for colectomy, suggesting that the appendiceal microbial reservoir plays a protective role against recurrent CDI.

**Parkinson's disease.** The discovery that the appendix contains abundant intraneuronal α-synuclein aggregates—including truncation products known to accumulate in Lewy bodies—has implicated the appendix in Parkinson's disease (PD) pathogenesis via the gut-brain axis. Killinger et al. demonstrated a 19.3% reduction in PD risk among appendectomized individuals over a 52-year follow-up period, proposing that appendectomy removes a pathogenic reservoir of α-synuclein. A Swedish register-based study similarly reported a 16% lower risk of PD following appendectomy (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.80–0.88). However, meta-analyses have yielded conflicting results, with some finding no significant association (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.87–1.20), and the most recent comprehensive meta-analysis (2024) also concluding no association (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90–1.12). The heterogeneity in follow-up duration and study design continues to fuel this debate.

**Type 2 diabetes mellitus.** If the appendix modulates immunity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has an inflammatory pathogenesis, a link between appendectomy and T2DM development is biologically plausible. However, only two cohort studies have specifically addressed this association. Wei et al. reported an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.3 for new-onset T2DM after appendectomy, with higher risk associated with perforated appendicitis. Lee et al. found a positive correlation only in patients younger than 30 years. A recent systematic review categorized the appendectomy-T2DM association as "unlikely," though this conclusion was based on limited evidence. The potential role of shared risk factors, including IL-6 gene polymorphism, has been proposed but not conclusively established.

**Cardiovascular disease, malignant neoplasia, and other conditions.** Appendectomy has been associated with increased cardiovascular risk (HR 1.35–1.58), although the contribution of surgical stress versus appendix removal per se remains unclear. Evidence for an association with malignant neoplasia is inconsistent, potentially biased by surveillance effects and short follow-up periods. Recent evidence suggests that appendectomy may worsen colorectal cancer prognosis through depletion of M1 macrophages. Associations with gallstone formation and systemic lupus erythematosus have also been reported but require further confirmation.

### Gaps in Current Knowledge

Despite substantial epidemiological investigation, several critical gaps persist in our understanding of the long-term consequences of appendectomy.

First, **the existing literature is fragmented**, with individual studies typically examining single disease outcomes in isolation, precluding a comprehensive assessment of the overall immunological impact of appendectomy. No study has simultaneously evaluated both clinical disease outcomes and immunological biomarker changes following appendectomy.

Second, **biomarker-level evidence is virtually absent**. While epidemiological studies have established statistical associations between appendectomy and disease incidence, direct measurement of immune and metabolic biomarker changes—such as leukocyte differentials, inflammatory markers, and metabolic parameters—has not been performed in a large-scale, multi-center setting. Such data are essential to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the observed clinical associations and to test the hypothesis that appendectomy induces a systemic immune shift.

Third, **most existing studies are limited by single-center designs**, self-reported exposures, and inability to adequately control for confounding. The absence of standardized data models across institutions has hindered multi-center comparative effectiveness research in this domain.

Fourth, **evidence from East Asian populations is scarce**. The majority of large-scale studies have been conducted in Northern European or North American cohorts, and the generalizability of their findings to other populations remains uncertain.

### Study Objectives

To address these gaps, we conducted a multi-center comparative effectiveness study leveraging the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) across 16 Korean healthcare institutions participating in the Federated Network (feedernet) research network. Using individual patient-level data (IPD) pooled analysis with propensity score matching and site-stratified Cox regression, we aimed to:

1. **Comprehensively evaluate the association between appendectomy and 11 clinical outcomes** previously implicated in the literature, including IBD, CDI, PD, T2DM, cardiovascular disease, and malignant neoplasia, using three active comparator groups (cholecystectomy, hernia repair, and general population);

2. **Investigate biomarker-level immunological changes** following appendectomy through analysis of 20 laboratory-based outcomes spanning hematologic, inflammatory, metabolic, hepatic, and renal domains, to test the hypothesis that appendectomy induces a measurable immune shift; and

3. **Validate and extend the findings of prior systematic reviews** by providing standardized, multi-center evidence from an East Asian population using rigorous causal inference methodology.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously assess clinical disease outcomes and immunological biomarker changes following appendectomy in a large-scale, multi-center, IPD pooled analysis framework.
